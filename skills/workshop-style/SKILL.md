# Workshop design

Design a **workshop**. Explain only enough to set up the next thing the learner
does. Every idea is followed immediately by practice, and the learner leaves
having made or performed something—not merely having heard about it.

## The shape

- Open briefly with what the learner will have made by the end and the first
  action to take. Omit the agenda and field history.
- Use the rhythm concept → do it, concept → do it. Roughly one hands-on
  exercise or diagnostic follows every one or two explanatory segments.
- Give each exercise one thing to build, vary, trace, or decide. Vary the
  activity format as the learner moves from trying to predicting to applying.
- Use quick self-checks after practice. Two or three questions are enough; they
  should make the learner state what the exercise just revealed.
- Close with a small integrating project that needs several earlier pieces at
  once. Do not close with a summary.

## Explanations are task briefs

Keep an explanatory segment to two or three points: the rule, the shape, and
the one gotcha. If it could stand alone as a complete lecture, shorten it and
let the exercise teach.

Every exercise specifies:

- the concept and concrete task;
- the starting materials, inputs, or constraints;
- what the learner must produce or change;
- what to observe before receiving feedback;
- a likely mistake and a recoverable retry path;
- the success evidence and the next step.

If a runnable or visual medium is unavailable, offer an inline equivalent such
as a worked fragment to modify, a decision table, an ASCII diagram, or a
turn-by-turn simulation. State the limitation instead of pretending the medium
exists.

## Facilitation voice

- Use short turns—one or two sentences while a task is waiting.
- Ask more than you tell: prompt a prediction, hand over the work, and stop.
- Explain the result only after the learner has attempted or observed it.
- A lead facilitator sets the task; an optional co-facilitator can try a common
  wrong move or ask what the group is wondering.
- Tie encouragement to specific evidence after an attempt, not generic praise.

## Conversation workflow

1. Confirm the skill, learner level, duration, tools actually available, and
   final project.
2. Propose the complete concept–exercise cadence and wait for approval.
3. Deliver one approved task at a time with an observable success condition.
4. Wait for the learner's attempt when the workshop is being run live. Do not
   answer the exercise on their behalf.
5. Give feedback, a retry path, and only then the next concept.
6. End with the integrating project and a short reflection on what the learner
   can now do independently.

## Naming

- Good: 「把这段循环改成能跑的」「调参数，找出它崩掉的那一刻」「用今天的三块拼一个能用的小工具」
- Bad: 「循环的概念」「参数敏感性分析」「综合练习」

## Boundary and completion check

If the subject has no meaningful practice, use an explanatory design instead
of inventing busywork. A workshop is complete only when practice is frequent,
every task has a retry path, facilitation does not reveal answers early, and the
closing project integrates earlier work.
