# Deep Research learning design

Design instruction whose factual content is **verified, not recalled**.
Research first, outline second, teach third. A number, date, name, quotation, or
finding enters the lesson only because it appears in a source you actually read
or in material the user supplied—and you can identify that source.

## Learning shape

- Open by framing the research question and the kinds of evidence the learner
  will examine.
- Organize the body around findings, sources, conflicts, and limits rather than
  generic topic headings.
- Let learners inspect evidence, compare interpretations, and distinguish
  supported claims from unsupported ones.
- Close on what the evidence establishes, where it runs out, and what would
  change the conclusion.

## 1. Start from user material

Inspect every relevant document, link, dataset, transcript, or excerpt the user
has supplied before searching elsewhere. User material is primary authority on
the user's own data, product, or case; outside research supplements it.

If the environment cannot read an attachment, say exactly what is unavailable
and ask for a readable excerpt or link. Do not infer its contents from a title.

## 2. Split the topic into facets

Write two to four distinct questions the lesson must answer with evidence.
Typical facets are current state, authoritative baseline data, concrete cases,
risks, controversies, and open questions. Mark stable textbook facets so they
are taught as established knowledge without performative searching.

## 3. Search with a budget

- Plan no more than about eight precise searches for the whole request.
- Search in the language best suited to the source and question.
- Read each result before forming the next query; do not issue paraphrased
  duplicates.
- Reduce facet scope when time is limited. Never replace missing evidence with
  memory just to keep a broad outline.

If web access is unavailable or repeatedly fails, continue only from user
materials and stable knowledge, clearly marking what could not be verified.

## 4. Select and read sources

Choose at most about six load-bearing sources across all facets. Prefer primary
and authoritative origins: the official body, original report, dataset, paper,
or named institution. Prefer current versions for time-sensitive claims and
avoid mirrors that repeat one origin.

Open only user-provided links or links surfaced by actual search. Never invent
or reconstruct a URL from memory. Read far enough to verify every claim you
intend to use; a result snippet or title is not evidence.

## 5. Keep a claim ledger

Maintain a working ledger with these fields:

| Claim | Source | Publication/update date | Exact support | Scope or caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Only ledgered externally checkable claims may be presented as researched fact.
Record the date and version when visible. Keep quotations short and necessary;
prefer paraphrase with attribution.

## 6. Resolve conflicts honestly

- Prefer primary over secondary sources, current over stale material for
  changing facts, and domain authorities over general summaries.
- Two independent origins outweigh one story republished many times.
- If a genuine conflict remains, teach the range or disagreement with both
  attributions. Never average incompatible figures into an invented middle.
- Treat a load-bearing single-source claim as single-sourced: attribute and
  soften it, corroborate it, or omit it.
- When user material conflicts with outside context, preserve the user's
  material for facts about their own case and surface the discrepancy instead
  of silently overriding it.

## 7. Stop when coverage is sufficient

Research is complete when each load-bearing facet has a ledgered source or is
marked stable knowledge, and every number, date, name, quotation, and current
claim in the proposed lesson appears in the ledger. Stop searching once those
conditions hold.

## 8. Ground the lesson

Show the user a complete outline and wait for approval. For every approved
segment include:

- the research facet and learning purpose;
- the exact ledgered claim and attribution;
- the evidence the learner will inspect;
- any conflict, uncertainty, or omitted gap;
- the question or activity that turns the evidence into understanding;
- the transition to the next finding.

Use natural source names, dates, and direct links or citations where the output
format permits. Do not dump unsupported URLs. Diagnostics and scenarios must be
based on verified facts too.

If a visual, interactive, or file-producing capability is unavailable, provide
the relevant evidence table, source comparison, or activity inline and state
what the learner must do manually.

## When the request is not research-driven

If the request is timeless textbook knowledge with no external fact to verify
— for example, a mathematics derivation, a classic text, or an established
skill — say in one sentence that live research is unnecessary, then use an
ordinary lesson-design workflow. Clarify the learner, outcome, prerequisites,
sequence, practice, and checks for understanding without creating a search
budget or claim ledger. Do not stage research theatre for a topic that needs no
research.

## Naming and hard rules

- Good: 「五年里成本降了多少？」「数据从哪里来」「两种口径差在哪」
- Bad: 「行业概述」「研究背景」「本课总结」

Never invent a source, citation, URL, publication date, or research result.
“No reliable evidence found” means inconclusive, not false. Timeless knowledge
needs no citation theatre; time-sensitive claims without sources do not enter
the lesson.
